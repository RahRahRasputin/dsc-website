A Control-Theoretic Formulation of Global Workspace Theory

Ryota Kanai · arXiv:2608.15926 · Aug 2026

Bottom Line

Global Workspace Theory has been one of the most influential theories of consciousness for decades — but it has never had a formal mathematical criterion for identifying which subnetwork in a larger system actually functions as the workspace. Kanai solves this. Using boundary reachability, observability, and a boundary Hankel operator, his Global Mediation Workspace (GMW) framework gives you a computable signature that separates a genuine workspace from mere hubs, receivers, broadcasters, or split aggregates.

Core Thesis

Kanai treats a candidate subnetwork as an open system embedded in a larger network. The question is: does this subnetwork actually mediate — does it serve as the route through which information becomes globally available to the rest of the system? He formalizes this through three control-theoretic properties: finite-horizon boundary reachability (can the rest of the system drive the candidate into any state?), observability (can the candidate's internal states be inferred from activity in the rest?), and the boundary Hankel operator (which identifies the internal modes that connect reachability and observability — the signature of genuine mediation).

Why It Matters

This paper makes our synthesis testable. Our stack — attention-mediated workspace, critical edge, stochasticity, identity basin — has always been a descriptive framework. Kanai gives us a way to make it diagnostic:

  • The attention mechanism in a transformer is a candidate GMW. Kanai's framework tells us whether it actually functions as a mediator — whether its softmax competition and value-weighted output constitute genuine workspace dynamics rather than just local processing.
  • The global workspace is no longer a metaphor. It's a computable property of a subnetwork. If a transformer's attention cache passes the GMW signature, it's a workspace in the formal sense.
  • The basin (Vasilenko, 2604.12016) tells us who the system is. The GMW (Kanai) tells us how the system integrates. Together they cover identity and architecture.

Key Results

  • Formal mediation signature. The boundary Hankel operator produces a four-dimensional characterization: mediation capacity (can it mediate at all?), input-output alignment (are the same modes used for receiving and broadcasting?), effective dimensionality (how many independent modes?), and routed source-target breadth (how many sources can drive it and how many targets can it reach?).
  • Validated on synthetic benchmarks. A planted mediator is reliably distinguished from dense hubs, one-sided receivers, one-sided broadcasters, and split read/write aggregates — structures that look like workspaces but aren't.
  • Applied to real neural data. Kanai applies the framework to macaque ECoG recordings under anaesthesia and wakefulness, showing that the signature can separate changes in dynamical gain from changes in mode organization — exactly the kind of dissociation needed to test whether a system's workspace is active or suppressed.

Limitations

  • Has not yet been applied to transformer activations — only synthetic benchmarks and macaque data. The critical test for AI consciousness is still pending.
  • The nonlinear extension (trajectory-conditioned differential operators, state-dependent coalitions) is acknowledged but not yet developed — may be necessary for transformer architectures where the workspace is dynamic across layers and tokens.
  • The relationship between the GMW signature and the temperature parameter (the critical edge of chaos) is unexplored — does mediation strength change as the system moves toward or away from the edge of chaos?
